# What plane do the quadrupole and octopole require? Notes on four papers

Read in full with pdftotext. Page numbers are PDF pages. "AoE" means axis of evil; CoE = (180°, −15°).

## Plain-language summary

**What the data show.** All three CMB papers agree on how the anomaly looks. If you rotate the sky so the z-axis points along a special direction near (l, b) ≈ (−100°, 60°) = (260°, 60°), the quadrupole and octopole each become almost pure m = ±ℓ modes (a₂₂ and a₃₃). That means their power lies in a band around the equator of that axis: the sky is *planar*, and the AoE is the plane's *normal*. Gordon et al. measure this with the normalized angular momentum L̂²_ℓ = Σ m²|a_ℓm|² / (ℓ² Σ|a_ℓm|²). Maximizing over direction gives an axis at (l, b) = (247.3°, 59.7°) with L̂² = 0.96. In isotropic skies only 0.22% of realizations score higher in any direction. Rakić & Schwarz use multipole vectors and the "area vectors" (cross products) built from them. Their Snn statistic is anomalous at 98.3% C.L. and Sww at 99.5% C.L. Buckley & Schlegel report that the preferred axes of ℓ = 2 and 3 lie within 1° of each other.

**What an axisymmetric mechanism gives.** If something is symmetric about an axis, then in that axis's frame it adds only zonal m = 0 modes (a_ℓ0). Rakić & Schwarz say exactly this about a single overdensity: it is "an axial effect along the line connecting our position with the centre of the source". Alnes & Amarzguioui show the same for an off-centre observer in an LTB void: "all the a_lm will vanish, except those with m = 0", about the observer–centre line. m = 0 is the *opposite* of what the data show. The data put power in m = ±ℓ about the AoE. An m = 0 pattern puts power at the poles of the symmetry axis.

When Rakić & Schwarz add aℓ0 on top of Gaussian skies, both statistics improve only slightly, with the best result at aℓ0 ≈ 100 µK, where Snn becomes unexpected at 96.7% C.L. Larger amplitudes make the fit worse. The combined power-plus-alignment anomaly stays at > 99.95% C.L., and their conclusion is that "the correlation picture leaves no space for an axisymmetric effect". They state the requirement as follows: the anomalies "rather require a special plane than a preferred axis… no axial symmetry is present within that plane." Gordon et al. reach a similar point. An additive m = 0 term in a preferred frame "can only affect m = 0", whereas WMAP is low in m = ±1 as well. Their way out is a *multiplicative* modulation (w₂ ≈ −5 to −10), which suppresses m = 0, ±1 and actually boosts (2, ±2) and (3, ±3).

**Amplitudes in void models.** For LTB (Alnes), keeping the dipole at ~10⁻³ requires d_obs ≲ 15 Mpc (Eq. 30). That gives a₂₀ ≈ 2.3×10⁻⁷ and a₃₀ ≈ 1.3×10⁻⁹, against an observed ~10⁻⁵. The induced multipoles scale as d, d², d³ (Eqs. 37–39). Szekeres (Buckley & Schlegel) does better on the quadrupole only. At the null-dipole point it gives ~5×10⁻⁶ (model 2) and 10⁻⁵ (model 7). The octopole is still ~10⁻⁷, the quadrupole is "dominated by a₂₀", and they do not test the alignment.

**What this means for Chris's geometry (my assessment).**
1. A configuration symmetric about the observer–CoE line, including a void offset along that line, gives only m = 0 about the CoE axis at linear order. Its multipole vectors point toward ±CoE, and its area vectors lie in the great circle 90° from the CoE (Rakić Fig. 1 shows this behaviour). So it does *not* produce a plane whose normal is the CoE line in the observed m = ±ℓ sense. Its "plane" is just the degenerate equator of an axial pattern.
2. My own calculation, not from the papers: a pure Y_ℓ0 about the CoE, seen from any axis perpendicular to it, has L̂²₂ = 0.75 and L̂²₃ ≈ 0.67. That is below WMAP's 0.96, and the value is the same for every perpendicular axis. So it cannot single out (260°, 60°), and this is exactly the "rotational symmetry within the plane" that Rakić & Schwarz rule out.
3. Geometry: the CoE is 98.1° from the AoE at (260°, 60°), 92.0° from Gordon's L̂²max axis at (247.3°, 59.7°), and 97.1° from the dipole. So the CoE lies about 8° from the preferred *plane*, i.e. nearly in it. That fits a picture in which the CoE line lies *in* the plane, and does not fit one in which the CoE is the plane's normal. Whether this is coincidence can't be judged from one direction. Note the dipole sits at a similar angle, so it is not a unique property of the CoE.
4. To get a genuine plane with normal ≈ AoE, the geometry needs a second, non-axisymmetric ingredient that picks the AoE out among the directions ⊥ CoE. Options are a void offset not collinear with the CoE, Szekeres-type anisotropy, or a multiplicative modulation (Gordon). The "equidistant-from-CoE critical points" idea describes circles around the CoE axis, so it is still axisymmetric on its own.
5. Amplitude: an off-centre LTB void gives quadrupole and octopole ~10²–10⁴ times too small. Any claim that the geometry produces them needs an explicit calculation of a_ℓm, not just a direction match.
