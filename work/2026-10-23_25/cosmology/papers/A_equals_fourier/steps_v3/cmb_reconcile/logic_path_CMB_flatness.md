# Logic Path: CMB Flatness in an Off-Center Expanding Shell Geometry

## Purpose

This note records the path of reasoning that led from the original
question about alternative interpretations of the cosmic microwave
background (CMB) to the present question:

> **Given an independently fitted, off-center shell geometry derived
> from the Migkas Hubble-anisotropy data, can the geometry strongly
> modify matter motion while still preserving the extraordinary isotropy
> of the CMB?**

The important constraint throughout is that the shell geometry,
center-of-expansion (CoE) direction, and associated parameters were
fixed from the Migkas cluster/Hubble-parameter anisotropy rather than
fitted to the CMB. The CMB can therefore be treated as an external test
of the geometry.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1. Starting point: decoupling Hubble anisotropy from the CMB

The initial interest was in whether the CMB and the observed large-scale
Hubble anisotropy necessarily have to share the same physical origin.

The standard interpretation is broadly:

-   The CMB temperature field is primordial radiation from
    recombination.
-   The dominant CMB dipole is caused by our peculiar motion relative to
    the CMB rest frame.
-   Residual CMB anisotropies after dipole subtraction are very small,
    typically of order \[
    `\frac{\Delta T}{T}`{=tex}`\sim `{=tex}10\^{-5}. \]
-   Large-scale matter and expansion anisotropies are normally
    interpreted within a nearly homogeneous and isotropic cosmology.

The proposed geometry instead begins with the Migkas directional Hubble
measurements and constructs an off-center, approximately spherical
shell/CoE geometry.

The key methodological point is:

\[ `\text{Migkas data}`{=tex} `\rightarrow`{=tex}
`\text{fixed shell geometry}`{=tex} `\rightarrow`{=tex}
`\text{independent CMB prediction/test}`{=tex}. \]

The CMB was not used to choose the CoE or shell parameters.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2. Working assumption for the CMB dipole

We then adopted a simplifying hypothesis:

> Treat the observed CMB dipole primarily as our **local peculiar
> motion** within the inner/local region rather than as a direct
> signature of the large-scale shell motion.

The measured CMB dipole corresponds approximately to

\[ `\frac{\Delta T}{T}`{=tex}`\sim `{=tex}1.23`\times10`{=tex}\^{-3}, \]

consistent with a peculiar speed of roughly
(370 `\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}`{=tex}).

After removal of that dipole, the remaining CMB is extremely flat.

This creates the main consistency problem:

\[ `\boxed{
\text{The shell geometry must be capable of significantly changing matter motion without producing large higher-order CMB anisotropy.}
}`{=tex} \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 3. Massive particles and photons do not respond identically in an observational sense

Both massive particles and photons respond to the same spacetime
geometry in general relativity, but they follow different classes of
geodesics:

-   massive particles: timelike geodesics;
-   photons: null geodesics.

For slowly moving matter in a weak field,

\[ `\mathbf `{=tex}a`\simeq`{=tex}-`\nabla`{=tex}`\Phi`{=tex}. \]

Matter can therefore accumulate a substantial peculiar velocity over
cosmological time:

\[
`\Delta `{=tex}`\mathbf `{=tex}v`\sim`{=tex}-`\int`{=tex}`\nabla`{=tex}`\Phi`{=tex},dt.
\]

A photon crossing a static gravitational potential behaves differently.
It can blueshift while falling into a potential and redshift while
climbing back out. Much of the net frequency shift can cancel.

A changing potential produces an integrated Sachs-Wolfe/Rees-Sciama type
contribution schematically of the form

\[ `\frac{\Delta T}{T}`{=tex}`\propto`{=tex}
`\frac{2}{c^2}`{=tex}`\int `{=tex}`\dot{\Phi}`{=tex},d`\eta`{=tex}. \]

This established an important possibility:

\[ `\boxed{
\text{large accumulated effect on matter}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{equally large net CMB temperature shift}.
}`{=tex} \]

However, that cancellation is not guaranteed in an off-center, evolving
geometry.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 4. Quantum particle creation was considered, then separated from the geometric problem

We considered whether the CMB could be related to quantum particle
creation/destruction in curved spacetime.

Known physics includes gravitational particle production, Bogoliubov
mixing, Unruh radiation, de Sitter/Gibbons-Hawking radiation, and
related effects.

But ordinary expansion of neutral matter does **not** imply continual
photon production, and MOND does not contain a rule that low
acceleration causes ordinary matter to radiate a CMB-like photon field.

Therefore continual radiation production was removed as an unnecessary
assumption.

The cleaner question became:

> Given emitting matter/surfaces and the proposed spacetime dynamics,
> what radiation field would an observer actually see?

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 5. The single spherical emitting shell thought experiment

We simplified the geometry dramatically.

Assume:

-   one perfectly spherical shell;
-   uniform inward emission;
-   an observer somewhere inside the sphere but not at its center;
-   initially no relative motion and no gravitational redshift.

For a surface of uniform specific intensity, surface brightness is
conserved along rays in vacuum.

Therefore an off-center observer can see

\[ I\_`\nu`{=tex}(`\hat `{=tex}n)=I\_{`\nu`{=tex},0} \]

in every direction.

If the shell is a uniform blackbody,

\[ T(`\hat `{=tex}n)=T_0. \]

Thus:

\[ `\boxed{
\text{off-center position alone does not necessarily create a radiation dipole.}
}`{=tex} \]

This was an important result. Simple inverse-square intuition is
misleading for a continuous uniformly bright spherical surface.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 6. Rigid translation versus radial expansion

Next we distinguished two very different meanings of "the shell and
observer move together."

### Rigid translation

If the entire shell and observer share the same velocity vector,

\[ `\mathbf `{=tex}V\_{`\rm shell`{=tex}}=`\mathbf `{=tex}V_O, \]

there is an inertial frame in which both are stationary.

The common translational velocity has no observable effect on the
radiation field between them.

### Radial expansion

For an expanding spherical shell,

\[ `\mathbf `{=tex}v(`\mathbf `{=tex}r)=v(r)`\hat{\mathbf r}`{=tex}. \]

Different parts of the shell have different velocity **directions**,
even if they all have the same speed.

An off-center observer can be locally comoving with nearby shell
material but cannot be comoving with the entire spherical surface.

Therefore radial expansion can create an angular Doppler pattern even
when the observer participates in the expansion.

This became the first serious threat to CMB flatness.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 7. First special-relativistic expanding-shell toy calculation

A simplified SR calculation was performed using approximately the fitted
present-day geometry:

\[ D=32.9 {`\rm Gpc`{=tex}}, `\qquad`{=tex}
R\_{`\rm out`{=tex}}=49.7 {`\rm Gpc`{=tex}}. \]

Thus

\[ `\frac{D}{R_{\rm out}}`{=tex}`\approx0.662`{=tex}. \]

A Hubble-like radial velocity field was imposed,

\[ v(r)=kr, \]

with the observer participating in the same field.

The angular temperature field was decomposed as

\[ `\frac{T(\theta)}{\bar T}`{=tex} = 1+
A_1P_1(`\cos`{=tex}`\theta`{=tex}) + A_2P_2(`\cos`{=tex}`\theta`{=tex})
+`\cdots`{=tex}. \]

For an illustrative outer-shell speed of

\[ v\_{`\rm out`{=tex}}=0.01c, \]

the toy calculation gave approximately

\[ A_1`\approx6.62`{=tex}`\times10`{=tex}\^{-3}, \]

and

\[ A_2`\approx-1.68`{=tex}`\times10`{=tex}\^{-3}. \]

The quadrupole was far larger than the approximate CMB residual scale of
(10\^{-5}).

This showed:

\[ `\boxed{
\text{a simple off-center expanding luminous shell in SR does not automatically preserve CMB flatness.}
}`{=tex} \]

But this was explicitly recognized as a toy model, not yet the actual
relativistic shell cosmology.

## 8. What "preserving the CMB" requires

The dipole and higher multipoles have different significance.

If the observed dipole is attributed to local peculiar motion, then
ideally the shell contributes little additional dipole:

\[ A\_{1,`\rm shell`{=tex}}`\approx0`{=tex}. \]

More importantly, the shell must not generate excessive quadrupole and
higher multipoles.

As a first-order scale,

\[ \|A\_{`\ell`{=tex},`\rm shell`{=tex}}\|`\lesssim10`{=tex}\^{-5},
`\qquad `{=tex}`\ell`{=tex}`\ge2`{=tex}, \]

is a useful target, although an eventual test should compare the
predicted individual (C\_`\ell`{=tex}) values with the measured CMB
spectrum rather than imposing a universal hard cutoff.

The important point is:

\[ `\boxed{
A_2,A_3,A_4,\ldots
\text{ are harder to hide than a dipole.}
}`{=tex} \]

A dipole can potentially be confused with peculiar velocity; higher
multipoles cannot simply be transformed away.

## 9. Adding the second shell

The two-shell geometry was then restored using approximately

\[ R\_{`\rm in`{=tex}}=18.1 {`\rm Gpc`{=tex}}, \]

\[ D=32.9 {`\rm Gpc`{=tex}}, \]

\[ R\_{`\rm out`{=tex}}=49.7 {`\rm Gpc`{=tex}}. \]

In the same instantaneous SR ray-tracing toy model, a sightline was
assigned to the first shell it encountered.

Because the observer lies outside the inner sphere, only directions
within a cone toward the CoE intersect the inner shell.

The approximate half-angle is

\[ `\sin`{=tex}`\alpha`{=tex}=`\frac{R_{\rm in}}{D}`{=tex}, \]

giving

\[ `\alpha`{=tex}`\approx33.4`{=tex}\^`\circ`{=tex}. \]

Thus the inner sphere occupies only a limited circular region of the
observer's sky in this instantaneous geometric picture.

The two-shell model reduced the toy-model dipole in some cases but did
not remove the quadrupole. The higher-order anisotropy remained too
large.

That result suggested the difficulty was not simply the number of
surfaces.

## 10. Critical correction: present-day ray tracing is not the CMB problem

A major conceptual correction followed.

The previous toy calculations effectively asked:

> From the observer's position **today**, what present-day shell surface
> does a straight ray intersect?

But CMB photons are not emitted by the shells in their present-day
positions.

The physically relevant object is:

\[ `\boxed{
\text{observer's past light cone}
\cap
\text{recombination hypersurface}.
}`{=tex} \]

If today

\[ D_0=32.9 {`\rm Gpc`{=tex}}, \]

the observer's position at recombination was

\[ D\_{`\rm rec`{=tex}}, \]

which need not remotely equal (D_0).

Likewise,

\[ R\_{`\rm in,rec`{=tex}}`\neq `{=tex}R\_{`\rm in,0`{=tex}}, \]

and

\[ R\_{`\rm out,rec`{=tex}}`\neq `{=tex}R\_{`\rm out,0`{=tex}}. \]

Therefore the apparent present-day distance to the "far side" of a shell
does not determine whether photons from that material can reach us.

The correct calculation requires the worldlines

\[ D(t),`\qquad`{=tex} R\_{`\rm in`{=tex}}(t),`\qquad`{=tex}
R\_{`\rm out`{=tex}}(t) \]

and backward null geodesics from the observer to recombination.

This may change the anisotropy prediction drastically.

## 11. Why the recombination geometry may matter more than today's geometry

Today's off-center ratio is large:

\[ `\frac{D_0}{R_{\rm out,0}}`{=tex} = `\frac{32.9}{49.7}`{=tex}
`\approx0.662`{=tex}. \]

But the CMB may instead be controlled by something like

\[ `\epsilon`{=tex}\_{`\rm rec`{=tex}} =
`\frac{D_{\rm rec}}{R_{\rm rec}}`{=tex}. \]

If

\[ `\epsilon`{=tex}\_{`\rm rec`{=tex}}`\ll1`{=tex}, \]

then at recombination the observer's worldline could have been much
closer to the effective center relative to the curvature radius.

Consequently, a strongly off-center present-day observer does not
necessarily imply a strongly anisotropic CMB.

This shifted the problem from **present geometry** to **evolution of
geometry**.

## 12. Parallel plates as the zero-curvature limit

To understand the role of curvature separately from dynamics, we
introduced a useful limiting construction.

Two concentric spherical surfaces approach parallel planes as their
common radius of curvature tends to infinity:

\[ R`\rightarrow`{=tex}`\infty`{=tex}. \]

Define the separation between the two surfaces as

\[ W=R_2-R_1. \]

Then consider three possibilities:

\[ `\dot `{=tex}W=0 \]

constant separation,

\[ `\dot `{=tex}W\>0 \]

increasing separation, and

\[ `\dot `{=tex}W\<0 \]

decreasing separation.

This allows three effects to be separated conceptually:

\[ `\boxed{
\text{curvature}
+
\text{bulk motion}
+
\text{changing separation}.
}`{=tex} \]

The perfectly parallel, identical, comoving, constant-separation case is
the natural isotropic limit:

\[ A_1=A_2=A_3=`\cdots=0`{=tex}. \]

## 13. Curvature sweep

A toy curvature sweep was then considered while keeping the
observer-to-surface distances approximately equal to the present values:

\[ d\_{`\rm inner`{=tex}}=14.8 {`\rm Gpc`{=tex}}, \]

\[ d\_{`\rm outer`{=tex}}=16.8 {`\rm Gpc`{=tex}}. \]

The common center of curvature was moved progressively farther away.

If its distance from the observer is (X), the surfaces were represented
schematically as

\[ R\_{`\rm inner`{=tex}}=X-14.8, \]

\[ R\_{`\rm outer`{=tex}}=X+16.8. \]

Thus

\[ X`\rightarrow`{=tex}`\infty`{=tex} \]

is the parallel-plate limit, while

\[ X=32.9 {`\rm Gpc`{=tex}} \]

corresponds roughly to the present spherical geometry.

In the simplified constant-separation SR model, the qualitative result
was:

\[ X`\rightarrow`{=tex}`\infty`{=tex}
`\quad`{=tex}`\Rightarrow`{=tex}`\quad`{=tex}
A_1,A_2`\rightarrow0`{=tex}. \]

As curvature increased, higher multipoles grew.

This established the key qualitative relationship:

\[ `\boxed{
\text{parallel geometry + common motion + constant separation}
\rightarrow
\text{nearly isotropic radiation},
}`{=tex} \]

while

\[ `\boxed{
\text{increasing curvature and/or differential surface motion}
\rightarrow
\text{increasing angular anisotropy}.
}`{=tex} \]

Changing the surface separation also generated anisotropy even close to
the parallel limit.

## 14. Current interpretation

The most important conclusion at the present stage is **not** that the
model fails the CMB test.

The calculations so far were deliberately simplified instantaneous SR
models. They do not include:

-   the actual relativistic metric of the proposed geometry;
-   the gravitational potential and its evolution;
-   the observer's worldline back to recombination;
-   shell worldlines back to recombination;
-   null geodesics through that evolving spacetime;
-   the physical recombination hypersurface;
-   gravitational redshift;
-   integrated Sachs-Wolfe/Rees-Sciama contributions;
-   a realistic CMB temperature and polarization field.

What the toy models established is narrower:

1.  **Off-center geometry alone does not necessarily destroy radiation
    isotropy.**
2.  **Common rigid translation does not matter.**
3.  **Radial expansion is not equivalent to common translation.**
4.  **Curvature plus radial velocity geometry can generate large higher
    multipoles.**
5.  **Changing separation between emitting surfaces can also generate
    anisotropy.**
6.  **The parallel, constant-separation configuration is an isotropic
    limiting case.**
7.  **The relevant curvature for the CMB may be the curvature at
    recombination, not today's curvature.**
8.  **Present-day shell intersections are not substitutes for a
    past-light-cone calculation.**

## 17. Why this is a useful test of the theory

Because the CoE direction and shell geometry were determined
independently from the Migkas Hubble-anisotropy data, the CMB provides
an unusually clean external constraint.

The strongest version of the test is therefore not:

> Can the model be adjusted to reproduce the CMB?

It is:

> **Given the geometry already fixed by the Migkas data, what CMB does
> the model predict?**

If the independently fixed geometry naturally produces a nearly
isotropic CMB after accounting for local peculiar motion, that would be
a nontrivial consistency result.

If it inevitably generates quadrupole and higher multipoles far larger
than observed, the CMB would strongly constrain or exclude that version
of the dynamics.

The immediate unknown determining which outcome occurs is the
**evolution of the observer and shell geometry between recombination and
the present**.
