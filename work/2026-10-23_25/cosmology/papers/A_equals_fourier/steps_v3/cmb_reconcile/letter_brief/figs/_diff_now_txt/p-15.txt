4    Discussion
The Migkas anisotropy is interpreted here as a gravitational effect: free-fall relative to an off-centre CoE. On
the last-scattering sky that same gravity produces a temperature multipole. Separately, a pure electromagnetic
Doppler pattern—from radial motion of emitting surfaces—has nearly the same angular shape. The kill function
is precisely that electromagnetic temperature multipole written opposite in sign to the gravitational residual.
The result of the present stress test is that this gravitational kill tracks the electromagnetic Doppler morphology
across the distances scanned. That morphological agreement is unexpected if gravity and the photon bath are
unrelated, and it suggests a geometric route to CMB flatness that does not discard the Migkas fit. The open
light-cone problem is whether that morphological match can be driven to the 10−5 amplitude required by Planck
once gravitational and kinematic contributions are integrated together along null geodesics.
    The apparent accelerated expansion of the late universe is, in this geometry, an illusion. No dark-energy
component and no actual outward acceleration of the material shell are required. What looks like acceleration
is a geometric artefact of off-centre sampling of the expansion field by an observer at distance D from the CoE.
A contracting universe under analogous off-centre and light-cone sampling would also present, on average, the
appearance of accelerated expansion, but with a different angular signature on the sky. That difference is a
natural distinguisher for future comparison against directional Hubble and supernova data.
    In the donut-universe time evolution, VHB = 0 until US passes Drecomb ; afterward the visible sphere grows
at c while the shells continue to expand faster than light in the coordinate sense used here. Volume fractions
VHB /Vann , VHB /VS1 , and VS2 /VS1 provide a compact language for tracking how the Hubble bubble fills the shell
annulus relative to the outer and inner spheres as the geometry evolves.
