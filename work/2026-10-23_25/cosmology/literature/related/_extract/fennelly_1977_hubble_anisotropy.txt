                                       Summary. The reported anisotropy of the Hubble expansion parameter
                                       is analysed within the context of a possible large-scale inhomogeneity. We are
                                       able to fit the data to a tilted self-similar cosmology with a Gaussian density
                                       distribution superposed on a homogeneous Universe. The particular model
                                       sets the inhomogeneous region’s diameter at 540 Mpc, with our Galaxy
                                       120 Mpc from the centre which is toward a = 12h42m, ô = + 20°. This elimi-
                                       nates the conflict between the anomaly and the isotropy of the microwave
                                       background.


                        1 Introduction
                        The expansion of the Universe has been generally considered to be homogeneous and iso-
                        tropic to high degree [1], as indicated, for example, by observations of galaxies [2] and
                        analysis of the microwave background radiation [3]. However, evidence on Sc I galaxies has
                        suggested that this may not be so on large scales (~ 120 Mpc) [4], and some confirmation of
                        this result has come from data on compact galaxies with absorption spectra and on
                        Markarian galaxies [5], the brightest cluster galaxies [5, 6], Type I supernovae [7], the van
                        den Bergh galaxy luminosity classification [8], and radio galaxies associated with galaxy
                        clusters [9].
                            More recently, a thorough analysis has established the existence of a Hubble parameter
                        anomaly more firmly, both for a variation with direction and an effect due to intervening
                        galaxy clusters [10]. Southern hemisphere photometry [11] has completed the original
                        Sc I map [4]. A complete sample of Sc I—Sell galaxies has provided a map of the anomaly
                        both for optical velocities and radio velocities [12]. Subsequent interpretations do not
                        agree [10,13], but application of one has substantially reduced the scatter in a published
                        Hubble diagram for nuclear magnitudes of first-ranked cluster galaxies [14].
                            An interpretation of the anomaly as due to a peculiar velocity of 500±125km/s of the
                        Galaxy presents a clear discrepancy with the isotropy of the microwave background radia-
                        tion [3], which limits our peculiar velocity relative to the frame of last scattering of the
                        radiation to 250km/s. This is a most serious disagreement within the context of what is
                        generally believed about the Universe.

                            Several interpretations and explanations have been advanced, ranging from the exotic to
                        the ordinary: One group of observers has developed a new physical theory requiring a
                        universal low-mass scalar boson field interacting with finite-mass photons [15]. This leads to
                        a static hierarchical Universe with ‘tired-light’ redshifts, in which photons traversing a cluster
                        of galaxies experience a greater redshift due to photon-boson scattering. It can explain one
                        interpretation of the data [10] but is close to an ‘ether’ theory of light propagation, and will
                        have to be tested experimentally before it can be taken seriously.
                            Other explanations involve selection effects or systematic deviations in populations or
                        intrinsic properties of objects across the celestial sphere. These seem not to be the case [13],
                        and there remain only dynamic explanations: a systematic peculiar velocity of our Galaxy,
                        a systematic anisotropy in the Hubble expansion (both of which can exist in homogeneous
                        Universes), or alternatively an inhomogeneity.
                            inhomogeneity of the purely Swiss-cheese type, affecting galaxies behind clusters of
                        galaxies can be ruled out [16]. Doroshkevich & Shandarin [17] have presented a model of
                        the formation of galaxies and clusters of galaxies that generates an anisotropy in H con-
                        sistent with the data. It is purely local due to the velocity perturbations induced during
                        galaxy formation and covers scales of 50-100 Mpc, with velocities 500-1000 km/s. A
                        peculiar velocity of the galaxy of 600 ±125 km/s toward a = 32° ±20°, 0 = + 530±ll° is
                        consistent with the data [13]. A systematic angular gradient in i/was rejected in [13] by
                        applying Occam’s razor and accepting the simplest explanation. There seems to be no
                        advantage in that anyway, since the conflict with the microwave background radiation
                        would remain: we would have exchanged a discrepant Doppler shift for a discrepant shear in
                        a homogeneous Universe.
                            But what of an inhomogeneous Universe? We examine here an alternative that is inhomo-
                        geneous and produces an angular gradient of H on the celestial sphere. The degree of in-
                        homogeneity is within the experimental errors of [13] for their near sample of galaxies (at
                        ± 3 a), which they used as evidence for a peculiar velocity. We are guided in this by the
                        results of Guthrie [6], which show the effect decreasing closer to us than -100 Mpc and
                        further from us than -140 Mpc.
                            Mavrides [18] has modelled a general class of inhomogeneous cosmologies which provide
                        order-of-magnitude arguments in support of this. He imbeds an Einstein—de Sitter universe
                        within a spherical vacuum region which is joined to an exterior Einstein—de Sitter model
                        using the Swiss-cheese junction conditions. The two Einstein—de Sitter regions have
                        different expansion rates and so their fitting must use an intermediate vacuum shell. A
                        Tolman—Bondi solution is given, analytically continued through the three regions. While this
                        is a desirable first step, it seems that a more specific model could be fitted. First there is
                        Guthrie’s result [6] which implies rather more structure than two homogeneous models
                        fitted together through a vacuole. Then an observation of Dodd et al [19] shows that there
                        may be a change in the number count—diameter relation in the opposite direction on the
                        celestial sphere, which may indicate a limit to this inhomogeneous structure at a particular
                        radius; in such a case there would be no intermediate vacuole, but rather a clump in a
                        Friedman background.
                            The Dodd result may not be statistically significant [20] but it does suggest a model with
                        a definite structure to be fitted with the data. First, the detailed nature of the reported
                        anisotropy will be examined and this will lead to a map of a possible inhomogeneity in
                        which we may exist. The structure of such an inhomogeneity will then be studied in a few
                        dynamical models. Finally its effects on the microwave background radiation will be
                        determined. Throughout the discussion we shall use the following terms in reference to
                        distance r: local for r -10 Mpc, large-scale for r -100 Mpc and global for r -1000 Mpc.

                        4 The microwave background

                        The conflict of the Hubble anisotropy with microwave isotropy may now be considered. For
                        this large-scale inhomogeneity the results of Dyer are applicable [29]. For a central mass of
                        ~1019Afo, there is a large redshift in the pattern centred on the inhomogeneity with
                        ôT- -0.3, and a hot ring (blueshift) of radius - 30° with 07=0.1 per cent around it. If
                        there is a central black hole of mass ~1O17M0 then the cool point at         13h, Ô - + 20° has
                        57--0.06 per cent and a hot ring of radius 2arcmin with 67-+0.03 per cent. Other
                        perturbation schemes produce variations of the same order. We see that such an inhomo-
                        geneous structure may just barely be reconciled with the isotropy of the microwave back-
                        ground radiation.
                            This should not be too surprising. The effects of a large-scale inhomogeneity appear in the
                        behaviour of geodesic congruences. The microwave photons propagate over global distances,
                        spending a relatively small part of their lifetime in the region of the large-scale inhomo-
                        geneity. Also their momenta obey pMpM = 0, indicating a much weaker effect on their
                        geodesics.
                            However, the galaxies spend their entire histories under the influence of local and large-
                        scale dynamics with their momenta obeying pMpM=~l. Their geodesics are therefore far
                        more seriously affected by the inhomogeneous region’s dynamics. Also, not only are their
                        images affected but their own motions as well, while only the image of the last scattering
                        surface of the microwave background is involved. A Doppler velocity of 500km/s [13]
                        would appear in both of them and apparently it does not. The inhomogeneous model
                        therefore explains the Hubble anisotropy and the background radiation isotropy. It is im-
                        portant to remember, however, that the microwave isotropy is not yet a whole-sky survey.
                        Therefore, anisotropies may yet remain to be discovered which would have to be confronted
                        by these models. It may well be that a significant anisotropy will require the addition of a
                        rotational velocity     component, or of some form of electromagnetic fields. On the other
                        hand such a discovery might also invalidate the models.
