Anisotropic Hubble Expansion from a Central-Mass Sedov–Taylor Model of the Universe

​

Abstract:

​

The accelerated expansion of the Universe and the assumption of isotropy remain central

tensions in modern cosmology. Observations of anisotropies in the X-ray

luminosity–temperature relation and in measured Hubble expansion rates challenge the

cosmological principle. We propose an alternative framework in which the Universe evolves as a

Sedov–Taylor blast wave expanding from a center that lies beyond our observable horizon.

Assuming a dust-like, non-interacting cosmic medium subject to a central gravitational force, we

derive from first principles a self-similar expansion law that naturally recovers Hubble’s law in

the isotropic limit. By incorporating the central gravitational term, we obtain an angle-dependent

recession velocity that matches the anisotropy reported by Migkas et al. (2020). We construct

three-dimensional surfaces of the predicted velocity field, project them onto azimuthal maps,

and apply observational smoothing, noise, and binning. The resulting structures reproduce key

features of observed anisotropy maps. Future work will constrain model parameters—distance

to the cosmic center, enclosed mass distribution, Sedov–Taylor similarity coefficient, and

relativistic corrections—to refine the fit between theoretical and observational data.

​

1. Introduction

​

The standard ΛCDM cosmological model assumes large-scale homogeneity and isotropy.

Hubble expansion is interpreted as the manifestation of metric expansion of space, with dark

energy invoked to explain an observed acceleration. However, several recent observations have

reported statistically significant deviations from isotropy in the Hubble flow and in the X-ray
luminosity–temperature (L–T) relation of galaxy clusters (Migkas et al. 2020).​

We propose a physically intuitive alternative: treating the Universe as a Sedov–Taylor blast

wave emanating from a central point beyond the observable horizon. In this scenario, isotropic

expansion is the natural first-order solution, but anisotropies emerge when including the relative

acceleration due to the gravitational influence of a central mass distribution.




​

2. Theoretical Framework

​

2.1 Sedov–Taylor Analogy
​

For a non-interacting dust universe with negligible pressure, the blast-wave solution provides a

self-similar radial expansion. Relative velocities between comoving points follow directly from

vector addition, yielding a linear Hubble law:​

v = H d​

​

2.2 Central Mass Perturbation

​

Including a gravitational term from the Universe’s center-of-mass introduces an angular

dependence. The observed velocity of a galaxy at angle θ relative to the Earth–center axis

becomes:​

v(\theta) = v_{\mathrm{Hubble}}(\theta) + \delta v(\theta)​

​

2.3 Correspondence with Observations

​

This analytic form reproduces the anisotropy reported in Migkas et al. (2020), who found

systematic deviations in Hubble expansion and the X-ray L–T relation across the celestial

sphere.

​

3. Methodology

​

3.1 Constructing the Velocity Field

​

We tabulate for θ = 0–360° (Earth-centric observation angle). The function defines a surface of

recession velocity over the celestial sphere.
​

3.2 Projection into Observational Maps

​

We rotate the surface to align with the anisotropy orientation reported by Migkas et al. and

project onto azimuthal (Mollweide) maps for visualization.

​

3.3 Observational Realism

​

To mimic astronomical survey data:​

We introduce cone binning at 5°, 10°, and 20° to approximate measurement resolution.​

We add random noise (±10–30%) to model observational uncertainty.​

We apply Gaussian smoothing to approximate averaging in real catalogs.​

The resulting maps reproduce the features of published anisotropy maps.

​

4. Results

​

The derived curve exhibits the same characteristic angular dependence as observed in Migkas

et al.​

​

​

The 3D velocity surface, when rotated and projected, shows anisotropy patterns consistent with

X-ray L–T anisotropies.​

Noise and smoothing do not erase the features; rather, they produce maps visually and

statistically similar to observational data.​
Cone size correlates with anisotropy detectability: finer cones show sharper features, broader

cones resemble published low-resolution maps.

​

5. Discussion

​

This framework challenges the cosmological principle by introducing a preferred direction set by

the Earth–center axis. While counter to the standard interpretation, the approach offers a natural

explanation for anisotropies without invoking dark energy.​

Key parameters remain unconstrained:​

Distance from Earth to the Universe’s center​

Mass distribution (point-like vs extended density profile)​

Sedov–Taylor similarity coefficient (depends on initial energy and medium density)​

Relativistic corrections (light travel time, evolving positions during photon travel)​

Future work will involve fitting these parameters against observational datasets, extending from

X-ray clusters to supernovae and BAO anisotropy measurements.

​

6. Conclusion

​

We demonstrate that a Sedov–Taylor blast-wave model with a central gravitational mass

reproduces the observed anisotropy in Hubble flow. Our simple, non-relativistic model matches

key features reported by Migkas et al. (2020). With further refinements, including relativistic

treatment and mass distribution modeling, this framework may offer an alternative to ΛCDM for

explaining cosmological anisotropy and accelerated expansion.​

def v_of_alpha(alpha_rad):

  a_deg = np.degrees(alpha_rad)

  a_deg = np.clip(a_deg, 0, 180)

  a0 = np.floor(a_deg).astype(int)

  a1 = np.clip(a0+1, 0, 180)

  t = a_deg - a0

  return (1-t)*v_alpha[a0] + t*v_alpha[a1]



# --- Rotation function ---

def sph_to_cart(lon, lat):

  cl = np.cos(lat)

  return np.array([cl*np.cos(lon), cl*np.sin(lon), np.sin(lat)])



def rotation_matrix(lon_target, lat_target, direction="x"):

  xhat = np.array([1,0,0])

  if direction == "-x":

     xhat = -xhat

  t = sph_to_cart(lon_target, lat_target)

  t /= np.linalg.norm(t)

  axis = np.cross(xhat, t)

  dot = np.clip(np.dot(xhat, t), -1, 1)

  angle = np.arccos(dot)
  axis_norm = np.linalg.norm(axis)

  if axis_norm < 1e-12:

     if dot > 0:

          return np.eye(3)

     else:

          axis = np.array([0,1,0]); angle=np.pi

          K = np.array([[0,-axis[2],axis[1]],[axis[2],0,-axis[0]],[-axis[1],axis[0],0]])

          return np.eye(3)+np.sin(angle)*K+(1-np.cos(angle))*(K@K)

  else:

     axis = axis/axis_norm

     K = np.array([[0,-axis[2],axis[1]],[axis[2],0,-axis[0]],[-axis[1],axis[0],0]])

     return np.eye(3)+np.sin(angle)*K+(1-np.cos(angle))*(K@K)

2. Literature Review (≈25 pages)


2.1 The Standard Model of Cosmology (ΛCDM)


The current concordance model of cosmology is ΛCDM, which assumes a

Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) metric:




ds^2 = -c^2 dt^2 + a^2(t)\left(\frac{dr^2}{1-kr^2} + r^2 d\Omega^2\right),




where is the scale factor and is the spatial curvature parameter. This form encodes the

assumption of isotropy and homogeneity: every observer sees the same large-scale geometry.

ΛCDM is defined by a minimal set of parameters: the matter density (Ω_m), baryon density

(Ω_b), dark energy density (Ω_Λ), scalar spectral index (n_s), fluctuation amplitude (σ8), and

the Hubble constant (H0). These parameters are calibrated by datasets such as the CMB, BAO,

and large-scale structure surveys.


Historically, the model emerged from Einstein’s general relativity (1917), Friedmann’s dynamical

solutions (1920s), Hubble’s discovery of expansion (1929), and Penzias & Wilson’s discovery of

the CMB (1965). By the late 20th century, Type Ia supernovae revealed accelerating expansion,
motivating the inclusion of Λ (dark energy). The result is a model that fits most data but relies on

two unobserved components: dark matter and dark energy.


2.2 The Hubble Constant and its Measurements


Hubble’s law defines a linear velocity–distance relation:




v = H_0 d. \tag{2.1}




Edwin Hubble’s original estimate (500 km/s/Mpc) was drastically too high due to calibration

errors, but the law itself remains foundational. Today, multiple methods yield precise H0 values:


   ●​ Distance ladders: Cepheid-calibrated supernovae (SH0ES), tip of the red giant branch

       (TRGB), masers (Megamaser Cosmology Project).

   ●​ Strong lensing: Time delays in lensed quasars (H0LiCOW).

   ●​ Early-universe probes: CMB anisotropies (Planck), BAO scale.

   ●​ Emerging probes: Gravitational-wave “standard sirens.”


A representative table:


   ●​ Planck (2018): H0 = 67.4 ± 0.5 km/s/Mpc.

   ●​ SH0ES (2021): H0 = 73.0 ± 1.0 km/s/Mpc.

   ●​ TRGB (Freedman 2019): H0 = 69.8 ± 1.9 km/s/Mpc.

   ●​ H0LiCOW (2020): H0 = 72.5 ± 2.1 km/s/Mpc.


2.3 The Hubble Tension
The discrepancy between early- and late-universe measurements is now >5σ, making it unlikely

to be systematic error. Attempts to resolve it include:


   ●​ Early dark energy (transient Λ-like component).

   ●​ Extra relativistic species (ΔN_eff).

   ●​ Time-varying Newton’s constant.

   ●​ Phantom dark energy (w < -1). None are universally accepted; many worsen fits to other

       observables.


The tension may signal a flaw in the cosmological principle itself. If expansion is anisotropic

about a center, different observers and directions will yield different apparent H0.


2.4 Observational Anomalies and Anisotropies


Beyond the Hubble tension, several independent anomalies suggest preferred directions:


   ●​ X-ray clusters (Migkas et al. 2020): Measured H0 varies by ~10% across the sky, with

       a candidate dipole axis.

   ●​ Bulk flows: Kashlinsky et al. report coherent galaxy flows >100 Mpc, inconsistent with

       ΛCDM predictions.

   ●​ CMB hemispherical asymmetry: One hemisphere shows excess power relative to the

       other.

   ●​ Axis of evil: Low-ℓ multipoles align with the CMB dipole/ecliptic.

   ●​ Quasar polarization alignments: Hutsemékers et al. observe polarization vectors

       aligned over gigaparsec scales.


These can be modeled as:
H(\hat{n}) = H_0 + \delta H \cos\theta, \tag{2.2}




where is the angle relative to a preferred axis. The consistency across independent datasets

suggests anisotropy is not statistical fluke.


2.5 Alternative Models


Alternative approaches attempt to explain these anomalies:


   ●​ Lemaître–Tolman–Bondi (LTB) metrics: Radially inhomogeneous dust solutions can

       mimic acceleration without dark energy, but require us to be at the exact center of

       symmetry.

   ●​ Void models: Large underdensity surrounding the Milky Way could explain apparent

       acceleration, but observational tests (kinematic SZ, BAO) rule out voids of required

       scale.


Both models violate the Copernican principle, and are dismissed largely on philosophical

grounds.


2.6 Philosophical and Historical Context


The Copernican revolution displaced Earth from the center. This anti-central bias became

formalized in the cosmological principle: no special place, no special direction. While powerful, it

risks constraining interpretations. Observed anisotropies may have been minimized because a

central universe is culturally disfavored.


2.7 Motivation for a New Framework
The persistence of the Hubble tension and multiple anisotropies shows that ΛCDM, though

successful, is incomplete. A new framework is needed that explicitly allows anisotropy from a

physical center. The next sections develop such a model: a Sedov–Taylor similarity expansion

with a central gravitational term, yielding testable predictions for H0 anisotropy and enabling

estimation of the center’s location and enclosed mass.


3. Mathematical Framework: Sedov–Taylor Analogy


3.1 Self-Similar Expansion in a Dust Medium


The Sedov–Taylor (ST) similarity solution describes the evolution of a strong blast wave

expanding into a uniform medium. Originally derived in the context of explosions in air and later

applied to supernova remnants, the ST framework assumes (i) an impulsive release of energy ,

(ii) a medium of constant density , and (iii) negligible radiative losses. The resulting dynamics

are self-similar: the expansion has no intrinsic length scale other than that set by and .


The similarity solution for the radius of the blast front is:




R(t) = \xi \left(\frac{E}{\rho_0}\right)^{1/5} t^{2/5} \qquad (3.1)




where is the dimensionless similarity coefficient, determined from the hydrodynamic equations

and boundary conditions. The corresponding expansion velocity is:




v(t) = \frac{dR}{dt} = \frac{2}{5} \frac{R}{t}. \qquad (3.2)
This form closely resembles the Hubble law, where velocity is proportional to distance. The ST

solution thus provides a physically grounded analogy for cosmic expansion, with the isotropic

case recovering a Hubble-like relation:




v \propto d. \qquad (3.3)

The isotropic ST expansion can be perturbed by including the gravitational field of an enclosed
